# KNOWLEDGE.md: what goes wrong, and what the app teaches

Source: the Hyle Deco submission, July through September 2026, every failure that
reached a Google Fonts reviewer or the designer's own eye. Each entry is a lesson the
app teaches, a check it runs, or both. The deep research corpus extends this with the
wider field; this file is the part that was paid for in blood.

Format per entry: what happened, why, the lesson in plain words for a user, and the
check. Lessons are written for a person who has never made a font.

## A. Outlines

### A1. Tracing destroys the drawing
What happened. The designer's Figma vectors were rasterised and contour traced back
into outlines. A capital T shipped with 1,763 points. The digit 7 had 862. The entire
font had zero off curve points; every curve was a polygon pretending.
Why. A tracer follows pixels and drops a point at every step. It reproduces the
appearance and destroys the geometry.
Lesson. A letter is a loop of points. A good outline uses the fewest points the shape
needs, and uses curved segments where the shape curves. A T is eight points. If your
tool gives you hundreds, it did not convert your drawing, it photographed it.
Check. Node count per glyph against a per class threshold. Off curve point ratio. The
count is shown at every stage, never hidden.

### A2. The "source" was a fossil
What happened. The UFO source shipped in the repo was extracted from the compiled TTF,
so it inherited the traced polygons. The reviewer's first line: one source, a Regular
UFO with TT curves.
Lesson. The source file is the file you drew in. If it was generated from the output, it
is a photocopy of a photocopy and a reviewer will see it immediately.
Check. Provenance: the app's UFO is always the origin, the TTF is always compiled from
it, and the app refuses to import a TTF as a source without labelling it derived.

### A3. Extrema off curve
Lesson. Every curve needs an on curve point at its topmost, bottommost, leftmost, and
rightmost extent. Rendering engines and hinting depend on it. A curve whose extreme is
between two points renders unevenly at small sizes.
Check. Extrema on curve, automatic insertion offered.

### A4. Coincident edges make notches
What happened. A hood stroke ending exactly on a stem's edge left a hairline sliver and
a visible step in the e bar and the a hood.
Lesson. When two shapes meet, overlap them by a few units so the union has no seam.
Never end a stroke exactly on another stroke's edge.
Check. Sliver detection after boolean union; overlap enforcement in construction.

### A5. Mixed cap styles on one glyph
What happened. The y had a round capped arm and a flat capped descender. The designer
saw "one side rounded, the other squared" instantly.
Lesson. Terminals are a font wide decision. Pick one style and every free end obeys it.
Check. Terminal style consistency across the font.

### A6. Diagonal tips overshoot the lines
What happened. The k arm poked 22 units above x height because the endpoint was placed
on the line, but a flat cap on a diagonal has a corner that extends past its endpoint.
Lesson. On a diagonal stroke, the cap corner, not the endpoint, is what lands on the
line. Slide the endpoint along the slope until the corner sits exactly.
Check. Tip alignment measured on the rendered outline, not the skeleton.

### A7. Arches at the wrong height
What happened. A rebuild set the arch of n, m, h, u to top out at 404 instead of 500
because an arc centre height was misread as a top height. u floated 96 units above the
baseline from the same bug.
Lesson. Every glyph's ink top and ink bottom sits on a metric line or overshoots it by a
consistent amount. Nothing floats.
Check. Ink bounds per glyph against the metric lines, with overshoot tolerance.

### A8. Overshoot is not an error
Lesson. Round letters (o, e, c, s) must poke slightly past the baseline and x height,
about one to two percent of the em, or they look smaller than flat letters. Flat letters
sit exactly on the lines. A tool that snaps everything flat is wrong.
Check. Overshoot present on round glyphs; snapping preserves it.

## B. Weights and styles

### B1. Inflating outlines to make a bold destroys the design
What happened. Heavier weights were generated by offsetting finished outlines. Counters
collapsed to slots (the Heavy e eye became 46 units tall), junctions grew lumps, digits
grew blobs in their counters, and every weight carried its own new defects.
Lesson. You cannot make a bold by making a regular fatter. Counters need to shrink less
than strokes grow, junctions need to be redrawn, and proportions widen. Bold is drawn,
or interpolated between two drawn masters, never inflated.
Check. The app derives bold only by offset from a clean skeleton with counters
re-solved, and labels every derived weight as derived until a human approves it.

### B2. Shearing outlines to make an italic distorts the stroke
What happened. The italic was the roman sheared nine degrees. Measured on the o, the
44 unit stroke swung from 40 to 49 around one letter, a 19 percent wobble. The reviewer:
italic is simply slanted Regular.
Lesson. Slanting a finished shape stretches its stroke. For a monolinear design the fix
is exact: shear the centreline, then re-apply the stroke. The weight comes back constant
(0.2 units variation measured) because it is re-created, not stretched. For a true
italic with different letterforms, draw it.
Check. Italic provenance warning; the derive tool only works from skeletons.

### B3. Google Fonts single style naming
What happened. A one weight family named Thin had to be renamed Regular at 400 to meet
the submission requirement.
Lesson. A family with one upright style calls it Regular, weight class 400, whatever it
looks like. The name describes its role in the family, not its darkness.
Check. Naming and weight class rules per family shape.

## C. Diacritics and composites

### C1. Baked composites, no anchors
What happened. Accented letters were positioned by copying bounding boxes. No anchors,
no mark attachment. The reviewer: the diacritics may need some work.
Lesson. Accents attach at anchors, named points on the base letter and on the mark.
Draw the mark once, place the anchor once, and every accented letter builds itself and
stays correct if you later edit the base.
Check. Every base and mark has its required anchors; composites are anchor built.

### C2. Composites inherit bounds
Lesson. When you edit a base letter, every composite using it changes. That is correct
and a byte diff will show all of them. Do not panic; check the base.

## D. Spacing

### D1. Sidebearings by class, not by eye
What happened. Measured against the o to o gap, straight stemmed letters sat 15 units
loose on the left and the whole diagonal family (v w x y z, f, s, t, k right, r right)
ran 37 to 43 units tight. The designer saw it in a paragraph before any tool did.
Lesson. Spacing is measured, not guessed. Straight stems get one value, round sides get
a smaller one, diagonals get less still. Test with control strings, nnonnonon and
HHOHOHOH, and a paragraph.
Check. Optical spacing assist by glyph class; control string proofs in the review.

## E. Identity and metadata

### E1. Colliding internal names
What happened. Two derived families both still identified internally as DejaVu Sans
with identical PostScript names. Android keys its font cache on internal identity, so
which glyphs rendered depended on load order. The same file was fine one day and wrong
the next.
Lesson. Every font file needs a unique family name and PostScript name in its own name
table. The filename is not the identity; the name table is.
Check. Name table uniqueness across the project and against system fonts.

### E2. Renaming a licensed base without attribution
What happened. Fonts based on DejaVu and Literata shipped without the required notices,
and DejaVu's terms require modified versions to be renamed.
Lesson. If you build on an open font, keep its copyright line, obey its licence, and
check for a Reserved Font Name before you rename it.
Check. Licence and attribution audit when a chassis font is imported.

### E3. Splicing foreign glyphs into a licensed face
What happened. Two geometric letters (N, R) were dropped into a serif face. In headlines
they read as errors. Identity was later carried instead by systematic details, square
dots and a custom asterisk, which fit any letterform.
Lesson. Brand a licensed face through details that repeat on every line (dots, tittles,
punctuation, one signature symbol), not by transplanting letters that fight the rest.
Check. Style consistency warning when a glyph's construction class differs from its
neighbours.

### E4. Counters are not dots
What happened. A dot squaring pass classified the eye of the e and the bowl of the a as
dots and squared them across 21 files.
Lesson. A transform that targets a class of shape must be gated by size and role, not by
roundness alone.
Check. Every batch transform previews its target set before applying.

## F. Process

### F1. Passing checks is not quality
What happened. Fontbakery reported zero failures on a font whose every letter was a
pixel trace. Node economy is not something it checks.
Lesson. Automated checks catch known errors. They do not measure craft. Ask what the
checks do not cover.
Check. The app states, on the results screen, what its checks do not measure.

### F2. Regenerating approved work
What happened. The designer approved a glyph set. Three rebuilds regenerated it from
formulas, each introducing new defects, discarding the approval every time.
Lesson. Once a human approves a shape, it is frozen. Changes are single glyph, surgical,
shown before shipping, and proven by diff to touch nothing else.
Check. Approved glyphs are locked; edits require unlock and produce a diff report.

### F3. Measurement that does not look
What happened. Contour counts and bounding boxes said fine while the letters were
visibly broken. Every correct call for a month came from the designer's eye.
Lesson. Look at the rendered word, large, on the device it will be used on. Overlay it
on a reference. Your eye is the final gate and it is usually right.
Check. Every stage ends in a rendered proof, not a number.

### F4. Ask what good means before building
What happened. Months of work before anyone stated the standards a reviewer applies.
Lesson. Before you draw the first letter: what does a professional expect, and what
would a hostile expert attack? The campaign's first task is exactly this question.
