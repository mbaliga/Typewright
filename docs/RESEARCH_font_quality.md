# Typewright must teach outlines, space, provenance

The domain knowledge Typewright needs splits cleanly into three tiers that map directly onto product surfaces: a small set of **hard, machine-checkable rules with published numbers** (fontbakery's six outline thresholds, the CFF 1,500-point ceiling, Google Fonts' vertical-metrics arithmetic, the `top`/`_top` anchor convention, the OFL/name-table string patterns), a larger body of **practitioner consensus that is qualitative but stable** (draw n and o first, space before you kern, never ship a mechanically sheared italic or an offset-inflated bold, place accents at the optical centre, treat Devanagari's headline as the alignment line), and a **frontier where no automated check exists** (spacing quality, curve smoothness, stroke consistency across weights, design coherence, whether a source is genuinely the origin of a binary). The most important single fact for an app that converts scans or drawings into fonts is that every widely used tracer, Potrace (GPL-2.0+), VTracer (MIT), Schneider's Graphics Gems fitter, is unaware of type-specific constraints (on-curve extrema, horizontal/vertical handles, integer coordinates, overshoot, path direction), so Typewright's pipeline must add those constraints itself and then run fontbakery-style heuristics as a backstop ([Selinger, Potrace](https://www.mathstat.dal.ca/~selinger/potrace/potrace.pdf); [fontbakery outline_settings.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_settings.py)). Equally important for the "Google-Fonts-quality" promise: Google Fonts requires reproducible builds from UFO/.glyphs sources via fontmake and explicitly refuses binaries exported from editors, and the QA tools (fontbakery, its Rust port Fontspector) check tables, metadata and shaping but, by Google's own statement, leave type-design standards to human eyes ([GF Guide: Libre Font Culture](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/culture.html); [GF Guide: QA tools](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/qa.html)). The report below is organised so each section yields lessons (what the user must know), warnings (what goes wrong) and checks (what can be automated), and it flags where practitioners agree, where they disagree, and where no guidance was found.

## Outlines: six published thresholds separate drawn glyphs from pixel traces

A well-constructed glyph outline is defined by practitioners qualitatively and by fontbakery numerically. The qualitative rules converge across Glyphs, Google Fonts, FontForge and independent designers: nodes sit at the x and y extremes of every path with exactly horizontal or vertical handles; every curve segment fits inside the "magic triangle" formed by its two on-curve points and the intersection of its handles; handles never cross each other or each other's extension; inflections are made explicit or avoided; all paths are closed, do not self-intersect, and run counter-clockwise for outer contours in cubic sources (clockwise in TrueType); no two points share coordinates; collinear points and handles on straight segments are removed ([Glyphs: Drawing good paths](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/drawing-good-paths); [GF Guide: Outline Quality](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/outlines.html); [FontForge Validation](https://fontforge.org/docs/ui/dialogs/validation.html)). The reason extrema matter is mechanical rather than aesthetic: hints attach only to on-curve points at extrema, TrueType bounding boxes are computed from point coordinates so off-curve extrema produce declared-versus-calculated bbox mismatches (Microsoft Font Validator error E1112), and extremum nodes make stem measurement and sidebearing setting deterministic ([GF Guide: Outline Quality](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/outlines.html); [TypeDrawers: bounding box thread](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/3659/are-off-curve-points-considered-for-the-bounding-box); [Letterpunch: font extremas](http://letterpunch.blogspot.com/2014/09/font-extremas.html)). This is **consensus**: every source mandates it, and Glyphs and fontmake can add extremes automatically at export.

The numeric thresholds that exist come almost entirely from fontbakery's `outline_settings.py` and are the safest defaults Typewright can adopt because they are the ones Google Fonts actually runs: on-curve points within **2 units** of baseline, x-height, cap-height, ascender or descender but not exactly on them are an "alignment miss"; consecutive line segments whose angles differ by less than **0.1 rad (5.7°)** are collinear; a turn of **≤ 0.25 rad (14.3°)** between segments is "jaggy"; a segment shorter than **3 units or 0.6 % of the contour length** is "short"; a line within **0.5° of horizontal or vertical** but not exactly on it is "semi-vertical"; and a check that fires more than **100 times** in a font is treated as design intent and passes ([fontbakery outline_settings.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_settings.py); [outline_alignment_miss.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_alignment_miss.py); [outline_jaggy_segments.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_jaggy_segments.py); [outline_short_segments.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_short_segments.py); [outline_semi_vertical.py](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/outline_semi_vertical.py)). Fontbakery itself labels all six as heuristics that emit WARN rather than FAIL, skips several for variable fonts and disables the semi-vertical check for italics; Typewright should preserve those severities and exemptions rather than promote them to errors. The only hard format ceilings are FontForge's documented **1,500 points per glyph for PostScript/CFF** and **96 hints per glyph**, plus a **32,767-unit** limit on coordinate offsets between adjacent points and the requirement that TrueType coordinates be integers; practitioners add that Windows has been observed to choke on quadratic glyphs above roughly **20,000 points** and that a 4,555-line glyph took seconds to render on a web page ([FontForge Validation](https://fontforge.org/docs/ui/dialogs/validation.html); [TypeDrawers: Too Many Points](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/4782/glyphs-with-too-many-points-possible-problems)). Thomas Phinney notes the 1,500 figure comes from FontForge's docs and questions whether the PostScript reference actually states it, so present it as a "risk threshold", not a licence to approach it.

No published table of "correct" node counts exists; this is a **gap** the research filled by measurement. Across 13 professionally drawn open-licence fonts (fontTools RecordingPen, closing point excluded), a sans-serif T is **8 on-curve / 0 off-curve** points, an o is **8 on-curve** (four extrema on each of two contours) with 16 off-curve in cubic and 24–32 in quadratic, an S is 12–28 on-curve, a g 17–35, an e 11–21; serif T runs 16–26 on-curve (measurement made during this research on 2026-09-22 with fontTools 4.62.1 over DejaVu Sans, Poppins, Work Sans, Outfit, JetBrains Mono, IBM Plex Serif, Lora, Libre Baskerville, Crimson Pro, TeX Gyre Heros/Termes/Pagella and Latin Modern; not a published figure). A pixel-traced T with 1,763 nodes is therefore roughly 200 times the professional norm. These figures are an empirical baseline the app can quote as "typical professional range", not a standard. The comparison must be format-aware: cu2qu, which fontmake uses to convert cubic sources to TrueType, defaults to a maximum error of **0.001 em (1 unit at 1000 UPM)** and tries 1 to 100 quadratic segments per cubic until the fit passes; at that tolerance off-curve counts stay roughly equal to the cubic count, while halving the tolerance to 0.0005 em raises them by about 25–50 % ([fontmake USAGE.md](https://github.com/googlefonts/fontmake/blob/main/USAGE.md); [fontTools cu2qu source](https://fonttools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/fontTools/cu2qu/cu2qu.html)). The pipeline implication is firm: fit cubics first (editable, fewer segments), convert to quadratic only at build time.

Overshoot is where scanned and traced glyphs most often fail silently, because rasterisation snaps round letters to the same pixel row as flats. Published guidance clusters at **1–3 % of x-height or cap height**, with Karow recommending 3 % for O and 5 % for pointed letters like A; FontLab states a typical range of **6–20 units** at 1000 UPM, larger for wide designs and closed shapes, smaller for condensed designs, and advises making all overshoots identical in screen fonts to avoid rasteriser rounding inconsistencies ([Wikipedia: Overshoot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overshoot_(typography)); [FontLab 8: Setting Your Letter Heights](https://help.fontlab.com/fontlab/8/tutorials/calfonts/3.%20Fitting%20and%20Spacing/03a%20Setting%20Your%20Letter%20Heights/)). Combined with fontbakery's 2-unit alignment band, this yields a clean two-sided check: a round glyph (o e c s O C S G) whose extremum sits exactly on a metric line has zero overshoot and will look short; one that sits 1–2 units off is a probable alignment miss; 5–25 units is plausible overshoot, 6–20 typical. No source gives separate values for baseline versus x-height overshoot.

### Raster-to-vector: every tracer needs a type-aware post-pass

Potrace decomposes a bitmap into pixel-edge paths, replaces each with an "optimal polygon" minimising segment count, then smooths corners using a per-vertex α with default `alphamax 1.0`, `opttolerance 0.2` and `turnpolicy minority`; output is cubic Béziers with the restriction α = β, and the algorithm is GPL-2.0-or-later with a proprietary "Potrace Professional" licence available ([Selinger, Potrace paper](https://www.mathstat.dal.ca/~selinger/potrace/potrace.pdf); [Wikipedia: Potrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potrace)). VTracer (MIT, Rust/WASM) is a linear pipeline with `corner_threshold` default 60°, `segment_length` default 4 px, `splice_threshold` default 45°, `filter_speckle` and an optional `--simplify` tolerance of 1–2.5 px claimed to halve file size ([visioncortex/vtracer](https://github.com/visioncortex/vtracer)). Schneider's 1990 Graphics Gems algorithm is the standard least-squares cubic fitter: chord-length parameterisation, Newton-Raphson reparameterisation limited to about four or five iterations, recursive splitting at the point of maximum error, G1 rather than C1 joins, and corners detected as a pre-pass and treated as segment boundaries with independent tangents ([Schneider 1990](https://lhf.impa.br/cursos/tmg/Schneider-1990.pdf); [GraphicsGems FitCurves.c](https://github.com/erich666/GraphicsGems/blob/master/gems/FitCurves.c)). The Graphics Gems repository EULA permits reuse but the paper carries no licence statement, so confirm against the repository LICENSE before shipping [CONFIRM].

The 2023–2026 research line, Optimize & Reduce (top-down parameter reduction, roughly 10× faster than prior optimisation methods), AdaVec at CVPR 2025 (adaptive control-point allocation via differentiable rendering) and AnchorFlow in 2026 (a learned sparse anchor field with a deterministic resolver), targets sparse, editable output and reports AnchorFlow at **61.2 parameters versus AdaVec's 171.2** on a single-path benchmark and only **+2.9 % parameter growth under boundary noise versus VTracer's +106.7 %** ([arXiv 2312.11334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11334); [ML Anthology CVPR 2025](https://mlanthology.org/cvpr/2025/zhao2025cvpr-less/); [AnchorFlow arXiv 2605.19551](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19551)). That last number is the single most relevant research finding for a scan-based app, because scanned glyph boundaries are exactly the noisy case; but AnchorFlow's and AdaVec's code availability and licences were not stated in the fetched sources, and no paper evaluates vectorisation against type-design criteria. None of the tracers or papers enforces extrema, axis-aligned handles at extrema, integer coordinates, overshoot or path direction. The recommended pipeline is therefore: trace or segment → detect corners (Potrace α or VTracer corner threshold) → additionally split at axis-aligned tangent extrema → fit cubics per segment with Schneider → snap extremum handles to exactly horizontal/vertical → round to integer units → correct path direction → run the fontbakery-style heuristics. Glyphs' documented cleanup order for imported art (Add Extremes → Correct Path Direction → Tidy Up Paths → Round Coordinates) is the same sequence in editor form ([Glyphs: Importing from Illustrator](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/importing-from-illustrator)).

A "pixel-traced glyph" detector follows from the artefact list: near-zero off-curve/on-curve ratio with more than ~40 on-curve points; runs of consecutive segments ≤ 3 units; a large fraction of segment angles within a few degrees of 0° or 90° but not exact; many turns below 0.25 rad; no extremum nodes at cardinal tangents; round glyphs topping out exactly at x-height. Any two together are strong evidence. Reasonable in-house warning levels, labelled as heuristics: flag a Latin glyph whose on-curve count exceeds about three times the measured range (over 60 for o, over 100 for g) or any glyph with zero off-curve points but more than 30 on-curve points. **No published tool scores curve smoothness or node economy numerically**; Speed Punk visualises curvature and Red Arrow and Glyph Nanny flag heuristics, but none produces a pass/fail threshold for curvature continuity ([RoboFont: Checking contour quality](https://robofont.com/documentation/tutorials/checking-contour-quality/)). Any node-economy score Typewright ships is its own invention and should say so.

## Provenance: Google Fonts accepts rebuildable sources, not editor exports

Google Fonts' definition of a source is operational, not nominal. Accepted formats are UFO, `.glyphs`, `.glyphspackage` (preferred over `.glyphs` for Git), FontForge and FontLab 7; anything else must ship a build script that converts to UFO; fonts are built with fontmake wrapped by `gftools builder config.yaml`; builds "must be repeatable", use open-source tools, run in one step, and live in a public VCS, normally GitHub ([GF Guide: onboarding](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/onboarding.html); [GF Guide: production](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/production.html); [GF Guide: build](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/build.html)). The rule that matters most for a conversion app is stated verbatim: "All Google Fonts projects must be built using a reproducible, libre toolchain. We do not onboard binaries exported from font editors," with the rationale that users must be able to "study the projects unhindered" in "complete 'source code' form" ([GF Guide: Libre Font Culture](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/culture.html)). SIL's development guide independently describes the same "canonical UFO source" model, with normalisation tooling (pysilfont) to keep UFOs vendor-neutral and diff-friendly ([SIL: Source Formats](https://silnrsi.github.io/silfontdev/en-US/Source_Formats.html)). The guide contains no sentence literally banning decompiled sources; the objection follows from reproducibility. A UFO decompiled from a shipped TTF inverts the relationship (the binary is the origin), lacks components, unremoved overlaps, kerning classes, feature source and anchors, and will not rebuild to the submitted binary, which is exactly the step reviewers perform. Critically, **no fontbakery or fontspector check inspects provenance**; a decompiled UFO passes every check its parent TTF passes. Typewright's lesson here is that "source of truth" is a property of the workflow, and its own export must be a real UFO/designspace with anchors, components and feature code, never a TTX round-trip.

Packaging is where first-time contributors fail most, and the failures are deterministic enough to prevent in code. Two public first-submission reports (Kumbh Sans, Noto Sans Buhid) show the same cluster: family-wide vertical metrics mismatches, missing nameID 13/14 licence strings, copyright notices not matching the pattern, OFL.txt body not matching the template, METADATA.pb subsets out of order or missing `menu`/`latin`, version not bumped, DSIG present, USE_TYPO_METRICS unset, invalid DESCRIPTION HTML, and glyph coverage short of the required set ([KumbhSans issue #1](https://github.com/xconsau/KumbhSans/issues/1); [google/fonts issue #4843](https://github.com/google/fonts/issues/4843)). The rules a generator must encode are concrete. Copyright string: `Copyright { year } The { family } Project Authors ({ git_url })`; nameID 13: "This Font Software is licensed under the SIL Open Font License, Version 1.1"; nameID 14: `https://openfontlicense.org`; fsType 0; family names with no camelCase, abbreviations, all-caps, hyphens or accents, and family plus style under **32 characters**; filenames `FamilyName-Style.ttf` (widths as `FamilyNameWidth-Style.ttf`, variable fonts as `Family[wdth,wght].ttf` with tags sorted); nameID 2 restricted to Regular/Italic/Bold/Bold Italic with other styles moved to nameID 16/17; weight classes Thin 100 through Black 900; a single-weight family that does not look Regular must be renamed "Family One" with style Regular; modified-semver versions that always increase ([GF Guide: requirements](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/requirements.html); [GF Guide: statics](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/statics.html); [GF Guide: METADATA](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/metadata.html)). Vertical metrics: identical across the family, typo and hhea equal, both line gaps **0**, fsSelection bit 7 set, win ascent/descent equal to the family's extreme yMax/yMin, the sum of ascender + |descender| + lineGap between **120 % and 200 % of UPM** (FAIL outside, WARN above 150 %), and never changed after onboarding ([GF Guide: Vertical metrics](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/metrics.html); [fontbakery vertical_metrics.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fonttools/fontbakery/main/Lib/fontbakery/checks/vendorspecific/googlefonts/vertical_metrics.py)). Reserved Font Names are discouraged because the API serves subsetted fonts that are legally modified versions, and both `metadata/reserved_font_name` and `name/rfn` checks fire on them; the OFL FAQ confirms RFNs are optional and, if used, should cover only the unique part of a name ([GF Guide: License file](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/license-file.html); [OFL FAQ](https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/)). Default to no RFN.

Glyph coverage is tiered and machine-readable: GF_Latin_Kernel (**116 glyphs**, minimum for non-Latin fonts), GF_Latin_Core (**324 glyphs**, minimum for Latin families), then Plus, Pro, Vietnamese, African and Greek, defined as YAML in the `glyphsets` Python package and enforced through `googlefonts/glyph_coverage` and `glyphsets/shape_languages` (which invokes shaperglot) ([googlefonts/glyphsets](https://github.com/googlefonts/glyphsets); [GF Guide: requirements](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/requirements.html)). Vendor the package rather than hardcoding lists. The upstream repository layout is fixed (`AUTHORS.txt`, `CONTRIBUTORS.txt`, `OFL.txt` with the copyright string on line one, `README.md` with an image and build instructions, `documentation/`, `fonts/{otf,ttf,webfonts,variable}/`, `sources/` containing `config.yaml` or `build.sh`, `requirements.txt`, `.gitignore`, tagged releases), and submission begins with a GitHub issue, "If your font isn't submitted through an issue first, your PR may never be merged", followed by a packager-generated PR that reviewers check for zero FAILs, justified WARNs, coherent METADATA/ARTICLE/OFL, and proofs showing no reflow, weight change, missing glyph, shaping regression or metrics change ([GF Guide: upstream](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/upstream.html); [GF Guide: Making a PR](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/making-pr.html); [GF Guide: Onboarder workflow](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/onboarder-workflow.html)). One open question: the packager creates `ARTICLE.en_us.html` while fontbakery's description checks still reference `DESCRIPTION.en_us.html`; which is canonical in 2026 was not confirmed [CONFIRM].

## Weights and italics: shearing thins verticals, offsetting clogs counters

The two shortcuts beginners reach for, shear the roman for an italic, offset the outline for a bold, fail for reasons that are geometric and therefore teachable with numbers. Glyphs' own demonstration shows that slanting "causes weight loss in vertical strokes while horizontal strokes retain their weight," with vertical and right-leaning stems thinning about **13 %** and left-leaning stems gaining about **16 %** at a deliberately extreme 30°; the sanctioned oblique range is **6–12°**, the slant origin should be at half x-height so italic sidebearings stay consistent, and extremes must be re-added afterwards because sheared curves no longer have nodes at their horizontal and vertical tangents ([Glyphs: Easy oblique](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/easy-oblique)). Even obliques get optical corrections in professional practice, re-weighted curves, rotated rounds, restored extrema, and Ilene Strizver's "fun house mirror effect" is the standard description of what an uncorrected shear looks like ([Wikipedia: Oblique type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_type); [CreativePro: Italic vs Oblique](https://creativepro.com/typetalk-italic-vs-oblique/)). Obliques are conventional for grotesque and geometric sans (Helvetica, Futura, Avenir) and inappropriate for serif and humanist designs, which use cursive-construction true italics with different letter shapes and widths. John Hudson adds a correction most beginners miss: because a diagonal between two heights is longer than a vertical, an italic's x-height reads optically larger, so it should be made slightly shorter ([TypeDrawers: How do I start working on italics?](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/2861/how-do-i-start-working-on-italics)). Practitioners **disagree** on whether a slanted roman is even a useful starting point for learning (Jasper de Waard yes; Hudson no, because cursive ductus is "a completely different beast"), and on whether a corrected oblique's extrema should sit at vertical tangents or stay slanted for interpolation ([TypeDrawers: Italify](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/5568/new-algorithm-italify-optically-corrected-obliques)). Glyphs' Cursivy filter and FontLab's Cursivying are the built-in corrections; practitioners call Cursivy "stem-info-based" with "all sorts of problems," and the geometry-based alternative Italify is available only as a paid service. A post-shear stem audit, measure stroke width perpendicular to the stroke at several points per glyph and flag glyphs whose range exceeds a tolerance, is implementable; **no source gives a canonical tolerance**, so a few percent is a conservative in-house value.

Uniform outline offset fails because it adds the same thickness everywhere: Design With FontForge notes that after an automatic weight change, sparse glyphs (1 i l I L j J) need to be heavier and dense ones (a e g x B R 8 &) lighter, and counters lose twice the offset while stems gain it, so joins clog and counters collapse ([Design With FontForge: Bold and Other Weights](http://designwithfontforge.com/en-US/Bold_and_Other_Weights.html)). Lucas de Groot adds that stroke weights grow slower vertically than horizontally and depend on topology (a single-stroke L versus a four-bar Euro sign) ([LucasFonts: Interpolation Theory](https://www.lucasfonts.com/learn/interpolation-theory)). The professional method is drawn masters and interpolation: "each significant change in weight will require its own master design," two extremes alone are "likely to be excessively bland," masters must be point-compatible, and the design space is described in a `.designspace` XML file (axes, sources, instances, rules, STAT) consumed by fontTools.varLib and fontmake ([Design With FontForge](http://designwithfontforge.com/en-US/Bold_and_Other_Weights.html); [fontTools designspaceLib](https://fonttools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/designspaceLib/index.html)). Spacing must also change with weight because "counterspace equals letterspace" and bold counters are smaller ([OH no Type Co: Spacing](https://ohnotype.co/blog/spacing)).

For stem progression across weights the field has formulas but not agreement. De Groot's theory is that optically even steps lie on a "hollow curve" below the straight line, with Regular verticals at 40 and Bold at 70, the SemiBold should be "slightly less" than 55, expressed geometrically as L = t·(b/t)^((x−1)/(s−1)); Impallari's hybrid weights the linear and geometric curves by position; Linus Romer generalised de Groot's curve with a parameter k = ln(y_B/y_A) ([LucasFonts](https://www.lucasfonts.com/learn/interpolation-theory); [TypeDrawers: Family Stem Weights Calculator](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/205/family-stem-weights-calculator/p2); [TypeDrawers: Generalization of de Groot](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/2665/a-generalization-of-lucas-de-groot-s-interpolation-theory)). The **disagreement** is real: Ben Blom insists progressions lie on or below the straight line and never above; Kent Lew calls stem assignment "an art, rather than science"; Jacques Le Bailly doubts the formulas transfer from sans to serif. The app can offer Lucas, linear and Impallari as calculator presets and warn when a user's intermediate stem exceeds the linear midpoint (Blom's bound), but must present the formulas as heuristics. **No source provides a table of typical stem widths per weight class**; only the worked examples (20→220 over nine steps; 40/70) exist. An "inflated bold" detector is feasible: compare counter areas and per-glyph darkening between regular and bold, and flag a bold whose dense glyphs darken more than sparse ones and whose counters shrink by the full offset.

## Spacing before kerning: n and o govern, and about 1,000 pairs suffice

Every source agrees on the sequence and the control set. Space the lowercase n and o and the uppercase H and O first, then derive every other letter by class: straight-sided letters take the n/H value, round letters the o/O value, diagonals the minimum, and a handful (a f g s t z; S) are set by eye ([Glyphs: Spacing](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/spacing); [n8willis/kernall: Tracy](https://github.com/n8willis/kernall/blob/master/tracy.md)). Glyphs' numeric starting points are the only ones found: the o's sidebearings roughly **one-third of its counter width**, the n's roughly **1.5× the o's**, flat bowls a little more and round bowls a little less, with the n shifted slightly right to compensate for its shoulder. Walter Tracy's method (learned at Linotype) formalises the derivation into five uppercase codes (same as H, slightly less than H, about half of H, minimum, same as O) and six lowercase codes (left of n, right of n, slightly more than left of n, minimum, same as o, slightly less than o), with the n's sidebearings as the maximum any lowercase letter receives; a comparison study found Tracy's method closer to professional originals for a sans and Miguel Sousa's shape-class method closer for a serif, and that sans letters need less space than seriffed ones ([kernall: Tracy](https://github.com/n8willis/kernall/blob/master/tracy.md); [TypeCulture: spacing methods](https://typeculture.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tc_article_49.pdf)). Glyphs' linked-metrics syntax (`=n`, `=a+20`, `=c*1.1`, `|` to mirror) is the natural way to express these classes in an app. The guiding judgement is optical: "counterspace equals letterspace," look for a "picket fence rhythm" of stems, and judge "three at a time", whether the middle letter feels optically centred ([OH no Type Co: Spacing](https://ohnotype.co/blog/spacing)).

Proofing strings are standardised enough to generate automatically. Paul Barnes' permutations sandwich each glyph between flat and round controls, `nnononoo nnanoaoo nnbnoboo…`, `HHOHOHOO HHAHOAOO…`, `00808088 00108188…`, while Hoefler uses `nnsnonosoo`-type strings and full proofs placing every letter at word start, word end and doubled; Hannes Famira's step-by-step letterproef runs control strings at **10, 12, 14 and 16 pt**, then real text in several languages, then exhaustive kerning permutations, then interleaved family comparison ([arrowtype/spacing](https://github.com/arrowtype/spacing); [Hoefler: How to Proof a Typeface](https://jonathanhoefler.com/articles/how-to-proof-a-typeface); [Famira: The letter proof](https://famira.com/article/letterproef)). Free generators exist for word lists built from the glyphs drawn so far: Word-o-Mat (RoboFont/Glyphs), adhesiontext, Idiot Proofed, the JAF generator, and the Impallari Font Testing Page (MIT code, CC BY-SA 3.0 text, runs entirely in the browser with fonts in localStorage) ([impallari/Font-Testing-Page](https://github.com/impallari/Font-Testing-Page)).

Kerning volume is the clearest place to correct a beginner's intuition. A "world-class" text face was cited with **750 pairs (345 after class optimisation)**, a classic display face with 890, and Lucida "functions effectively with no kerning at all," while Thomas Phinney recalls applications historically failing above 3,000–8,000 pairs; John Hudson notes that most Latin kerning involves diagonals and overhangs (T V W Y A L f r, quotes, punctuation) and straight–straight or round–round pairs rarely need any ([TypeDrawers: Efficiency in kerning pairs](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/2313/efficiency-in-kerning-pairs)). The one data-driven study (2,700 Google Fonts against ~1 GB of Wikipedia text in 24 languages) finds that roughly **1,000 pairs cover every context that occurs at least once per page**, ~2,000 cover once per ten pages, ~3,736 once per hundred, and only about 36 % of possible pairs ever occur in text ([andre-fuchs/kerning-pairs](https://github.com/andre-fuchs/kerning-pairs)). Simon Cozens warns against treating pair count as a quality proxy; Andreas Stötzner's rule is to kern only pairs that are "absolutely necessary" after meticulous spacing. Warnings that follow: more than ~1,000–2,000 pairs on a Latin text face suggests the spacing is wrong; kerned straight–straight or round–round pairs suggest the same; a kern value larger than the glyph's sidebearing suggests kerning is compensating for spacing. **No source gives a canonical maximum kern value** relative to sidebearings or em.

For automatic spacing, HT Letterspacer (Huerta Tipográfica; code GPL-3.0, docs CC BY 4.0) is the reference open implementation: it samples the outline vertically every **5 units** by default (`paramFreq`), computes the white-area polygon on each side within a reference zone, sets sidebearings so each side reaches a target area, and limits penetration into open counters with a "depth" parameter ([HTLetterspacer](https://github.com/huertatipografica/HTLetterspacer)). Version 2 is Glyphs 3-only, so Typewright would re-implement from the published description (GPL-3.0 if it reuses code); Area and Depth map naturally onto "tightness" and "counter openness" sliders. Its default Area/Depth values were not in the fetched material.

## Diacritics: anchors, not bounding boxes, and a 5–10 % of em gap

Accent placement has one mechanical rule that is not negotiable and several numeric defaults that are. The mechanism is anchors: base glyphs carry `top`, `bottom` or `ogonek`; marks carry `_top`, `_bottom`, `_ogonek`; the editor uses the pair to build precomposed composites automatically and to generate the GPOS `mark` (mark-to-base, lookup type 4) and `mkmk` (mark-to-mark, type 6) features; combining marks must have zero advance width and a `comb` suffix; Microsoft lists `mark` and `mkmk` as required for standard scripts ([Glyphs: Diacritics](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/diacritics); [GF Guide: Diacritics](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/diacritics.html); [Microsoft: Standard scripts](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/script-development/standard)). Positioning accents by bounding box produces none of this: no live composites, no combining-mark support (so `e` + U+0301 lands on the dotted circle or overlaps), no stacking (marks need both `_top` and `top` so Vietnamese stacks work), and no case or narrow logic. Capitals need `.case` marks that are "wider, shorter, and flatter," placed with the anchor exactly on cap height; i and j need `.i`/`.narrow` marks and dotless bases substituted through `ccmp` ([Glyphs: narrow marks](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/advanced-diacritics-narrow-marks); [GF Guide: Diacritics](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/diacritics.html)). These three defects, composites without anchors, no `ccmp` for soft-dotted i/j, no `.case` marks, are each detectable from a compiled font.

The numeric defaults come from Microsoft's character design standards and are explicitly "starting points": marks are placed at the **visual** centre, not the geometric one; the acute has one-third of its black width left of the base's centreline and two-thirds right, the grave the reverse; the gap between the bottom of acute/grave and the lowercase overshoot is "approximately **5 to 10 % of the em**," typically 100–200 units at 2048 UPM (Palatino Linotype: 98 over O, 130 over o; Georgia: 54 over O, 96 over o), with uppercase accents closer to the letter than lowercase ones; the ogonek sits on the right stem of A and slightly right of centre on round letters ([Microsoft: Diacritics](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/develop/character-design-standards/diacritics)). Weight guidance is unanimous: "Accents are word-builders" and must match the letters' colour, they should not fall off the base to either side, capital accents should be smaller and shallower, and a common beginner error is drawing accents too small for 10–12 pt or applying the letters' stress axis to them ([I Love Typography: On Diacritics](https://ilovetypography.com/2009/01/24/on-diacritics/)). Language-specific choices are checkable facts: lowercase t and d take the apostrophe-style caron while T and D take the full form; Romanian S/T want comma-below (U+0218–021B) while Turkish S wants a connecting cedilla ([Microsoft: Diacritics](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/develop/character-design-standards/diacritics)). No source gives a numeric gap for stacked marks, Google Fonts says only "tight and consistent", and no fetched source enumerates fontbakery's mark-related check IDs beyond the shaping checks (`dotted_circle`, `soft_dotted`).

## Multi-script: shaping rules are hard, metric ratios are absent

For all three non-Latin scripts the pattern is the same: the OpenType shaping model and naming conventions are precise and enforceable, while the vertical-metric relationships to Latin are qualitative and designer-chosen. Typewright should present templates with **[CONFIRM]** placeholders for ratios rather than invent them.

### Devanagari hangs from the headline

Devanagari has seven named vertical levels (urdhvarekha, shirorekha or headline, skandharekha, nabhirekha, zanurekha, padrekha, talrekha), of which only headline and baseline are fixed; there is no x-height, matras extend above and below and are not called ascenders or descenders, and "there is not and cannot be a prescriptive model" for where every part touches a line ([TypeTogether: Devanagari Type Anatomy](https://www.type-together.com/devanagari-type-anatomy)). Harmonisation with Latin has two documented options, Latin cap height comparable to upper-matra height, or Latin x-height comparable to the headline, with no percentage given; Devanagari needs more leading than Latin and tracking breaks the headline ([Alphabettes: Devanagari Typography 101](https://www.alphabettes.org/devanagari-typography-101-a-guide-for-typesetting-with-latin/); [TypeTogether: multiscript guide](https://www.type-together.com/multiscript-typography-guide)). Glyphs' workflow starts with `aaMatra-deva` (the vertical stem), then half forms, full consonants as composites, stemless "hanging" consonants, `halant-deva`, `reph-deva`, `rakar-deva`, several `iMatra-deva` length variants and `nukta-deva`; sidebearings go negative so the headline overlaps; names use the `-deva` suffix with conjuncts joined by underscore ([Glyphs: Creating a Devanagari font](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/creating-a-devanagari-font)). Design With FontForge's first-glyph progression (पाव → किमीनुफू → भरसगदह → height extremes र्मों ड्डू) and its spacing drill (pages of पप, then पप_पवपवXवव) are the most concrete pedagogy found, alongside its warning to "avoid cutting-and-pasting Latin letterparts into Devanagari" and its estimate of **4–6 months minimum** for light plus bold by an experienced designer ([Design With FontForge: Devanagari](https://github.com/fontforge/designwithfontforge.com/blob/gh-pages/en-US/Designing_Devanagari_Typefaces.md)). The shaping rules are enforceable: GSUB features apply in the order `locl, nukt, akhn, rphf, rkrf, blwf, half, vatu, cjct` then `pres, abvs, blws, psts, haln, calt`, GPOS `kern/dist, abvm, blwm`; the font must supply reph, rakaar, half forms, below-base Ra and the akhand ligatures Ka+Ssa and Ja+Nya; control characters U+25CC, U+200C, U+200D and U+200B are required; and "OpenType fonts should not have substitutions that attempt to perform the re-ordering" because the engine reorders before lookups run ([Microsoft: Devanagari spec](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/script-development/devanagari); [n8willis: Devanagari shaping](https://github.com/n8willis/opentype-shaping-documents/blob/master/opentype-shaping-devanagari.md)). Sources differ slightly on whether `pstf` appears in the Devanagari stage list (Microsoft omits it; n8willis includes it); treat it as present but rarely used.

### Arabic Naskh joins on a weighted baseline; Nastaliq exceeds OpenType

Arabic's metric system replaces x-height with tooth-, loop- and eye-heights, may use one or two ascender levels ("sky") and two or three descender levels ("earth"), and gives the baseline itself a thickness; a Naskh-inspired design may use up to **twelve** imaginary levels versus four or five for a geometric Kufic ([29LT: Arabic Type Anatomy](https://blog.29lt.com/2015/07/30/arabic-type-anatomy-typographic-terms/)). "Do not use 'x-height' and 'cap height'" in Arabic vocabulary ([TypeTogether: Arabic Type Anatomy](https://www.type-together.com/arabic-type-anatomy)). Classical proportions use the rhombic nib dot as a measuring stick and Lara Captan uses them "as a guideline"; the governing aesthetic is that "Arabic typefaces must never break the illusion of continuous movement" ([Communication Arts: Arabic type design](https://www.commarts.com/features/basic-principles-of-arabic-type-design)). The Glyphs recipe is concrete: start from `behDotless-ar` in all four forms (isolated, `.init`, `.medi`, `.fina`) because ب ت ث پ share a skeleton and differ only by dots; draw dot marks as separate glyphs with `_top`/`top` and `_bottom`/`bottom` so they stack; set a "joining line height" master metric equal to the stroke width (default 100 with 16-unit overshoot); place `exit` and `entry` anchors at **y = 0** and horizontally on the sidebearings because older renderers fail otherwise; make connector stroke ends click into each other with zero sidebearing rather than merely overlapping ([Glyphs: Creating an Arabic font](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/arabic)). The feature model is fixed: GSUB `ccmp, isol, fina, medi, init, rlig, rclt, calt` always applied, `liga` on by default, GPOS `curs, kern, mark, mkmk`; `rlig` must contain lam-alef; marks are validated in classes such that above-marks (DIAC1) and below-marks (DIAC2) of the same class are never doubled on a base ([Microsoft: Arabic spec](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/script-development/arabic); [n8willis: Arabic shaping](https://github.com/n8willis/opentype-shaping-documents/blob/master/opentype-shaping-arabic.md)). Lints follow directly: every dual-joining letter has four forms, every form has exit/entry at y=0 on the sidebearings, every base has `top` and `bottom`, `rlig` contains lam_alef. Urdu adds a hard limit the app must state honestly: the culturally expected Nastaliq style has a sloping baseline and position-dependent collisions that "OpenType can't express," which is why Awami Nastaliq ships only in SIL Graphite and why Simon Cozens' OpenType attempt needed multiple dot-height variants, a collision detector over three-glyph windows and "half a million cases to check" ([SIL Awami Nastaliq FAQ](https://software.sil.org/awami/support/faq/); [Cozens: Nastaliq Dot Positioning](https://simoncozens.github.io/nastaliq-dot-positioning/)). No source gave numeric tooth/loop/descender ratios in dot units, and none addressed Urdu-Naskh versus Arabic-Naskh design differences.

### Kana are centred in a virtual body, not seated on a baseline

Japanese characters occupy an invisible square "virtual body" (仮想ボディ) with a smaller "letter face" (字面) that governs apparent size; because kana have fewer strokes and wider inner space than kanji they look larger at the same face, so kana faces are drawn smaller, by an amount that varies by design and for which **no percentage is published** ([Type Project: Type Face](https://staffblog.typeproject.com/en/987); [AQ: Japanese typography](https://www.aqworks.com/blog/japanese-typography-on-the-web-and-beyond-part-one)). The Apoc JP case study gives the only quantified companion-design decision: kana were placed in a **980-unit** em box against a 1000-unit Latin to match texture, letters were centred like "pieces of meat skewered on a barbecue rod" using a Nine Box View with the Latin H as reference, strokes were drawn slightly thinner because "Asian characters tend to appear visually heavier than Latin if drawn using exactly the same measurements," and dakuten/handakuten forced structural changes to some kana because the upper-right corner is crowded ([Blaze Type: Apoc JP](https://blazetype.eu/blog/apoc-jp-making-of-japanese/)). Dakuten (゛) and handakuten (゜) sit at the upper right; they are encoded as combining U+3099/U+309A, spacing U+309B/U+309C and half-width U+FF9E/U+FF9F, with the combining forms required for half-width kana which have no precomposed glyphs ([Wikipedia: Dakuten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakuten_and_handakuten)). A complete kana set is roughly **200 glyphs**, not 92, once voiced, semi-voiced, small kana, horizontal and vertical variants, historical ゐゑ, the prolonged-sound mark and iteration marks are counted ([Type Project: Surprisingly Many Kana Glyphs](https://staffblog.typeproject.com/en/3458)). Template implications: an em square, a letter-face box smaller than the em and smaller for kana than kanji, a vertical optical-centre line rather than a baseline, overridable upper-right anchors for dakuten, and a planned `vert` set. No "draw these kana first" list was found.

## Anatomy, critique and sequence: n and o first, then families, then everything else

The anatomy vocabulary Typewright needs for an in-app lens is fully covered by two openly fetchable glossaries, Fonts.com's Fontology and Wikipedia's "Typeface anatomy" (which cites FontShop, Coles' *The Anatomy of Type* and Carter/Day/Meggs), while Google Fonts Knowledge, reviewed by 31 practitioners including Lupton and Spiekermann, with 100+ glossary entries, is the right primary link even though it renders client-side and could not be quoted ([Fonts.com Fontology](https://www.myfonts.com/pages/fontscom-learning-fontology-level-1-type-anatomy-anatomy); [Wikipedia: Typeface anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface_anatomy); [Elliot Jay Stocks on GF Knowledge](https://elliotjaystocks.com/blog/google-fonts-knowledge)). The useful structure is a term-to-glyph graph: ear, link and loop belong to the two-storey g only; spur to G and e; apex to A M N x and vertex to V W v w; tail to Q R j y J; eye is the closed counter of e; open counters (a c e f h m n r s t u) are where "aperture" applies; stem, bowl, counter, terminal, crossbar, arm, leg, shoulder, spine, tittle and swash follow the quoted definitions; stress is "the direction of thickening in a curved stroke" and contrast "the disparity between thick and thin strokes." Font-level terms (baseline, x-height, cap height, ascender, descender, overshoot, weight) should be guides rather than labels. Gaps: "aperture" and "hairline serif" are not defined in the fetched glossaries and no glossary separates "axis" from "stress."

Critique follows a recoverable diagnostic order even though **no single published rubric exists**. OH no Type's proofing method starts with running text at target size because "dark or light spots will attract your eye, and alert you to problematic glyphs," then spacing strings with n/o and H/O controls, then every glyph large enough to annotate, plus "Goldilocks" proofs of extremes (six paragraphs from 0 to +20 tracking) to locate a target; Famira ends with interleaved side-by-side comparison of family members; Ruder's paired paragraphs test that lowercase and uppercase texts match in colour ([OH no Type Co: Proof It!](https://ohnotype.co/blog/proof-it); [Famira: letterproef](https://famira.com/article/letterproef); [arrowtype/spacing](https://github.com/arrowtype/spacing)). Test words are not arbitrary: Hamburgefonstiv "contains all essential forms in a Latin alphabet" and "all of the curves and abutments normally found in a font"; "adhesion" is Reading's starter word and "videospian" TypeTogether's; Gerry Leonidas' Greek starter set is α ε η ι μ ρ ([Wikipedia: Hamburgevons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburgevons); [TypeDrawers: Control words](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/1052/control-words-in-different-languages-scripts); [Design With FontForge: Type DNA](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fontforge/designwithfontforge.com/master/en-US/Creating_Your_Types_Dna.md)). Public crit formats (Alphacrit's ten minutes per presenter with a specific question prepared in advance; TypeDrawers' critique category; Type@Cooper's regular presentations) all reward a focused question and a printed specimen ([Alphacrit](https://www.alphacrit.alphabettes.org/)).

Sequencing is the strongest **consensus** in the whole corpus. Design With FontForge begins with o (bowl weight, counter, overshoot, sidebearings) and n (stem weight, stem-to-curve relationship), refines and spaces them together, then derives h m u from n and b d p q from o, adds a d e h i s v as "DNA" letters, and warns that "rapid expansion before this is done will mean that the whole project will become harder to manage" ([Creating o and n](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fontforge/designwithfontforge.com/master/en-US/Creating_o_and_n.md); [Type DNA](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fontforge/designwithfontforge.com/master/en-US/Creating_Your_Types_Dna.md)). Practitioners vary the control set but not the principle, Hudson's i n l h m o I H O so real words like "nihilim" and "onion" can be set; Kaczun's H O n o 0 to fix alignment, stems, hairlines and spacing; Larabie's advice to draw the hardest letters (R, e) in the heaviest weight first to avoid painting yourself into a corner; Bloom's n o H O then Hamburgefonstiv ([TypeDrawers: Which letters first](https://typedrawers.com/discussion/1532/which-letters-to-design-first-to-base-others-off-of-it); [TYPE01: Mark Bloom](https://type-01.com/5-important-tips-about-type-design-from-mark-bloom/)). Karen Cheng's *Designing Type* orders serif caps, serif lowercase, sans caps, sans lowercase, numbers, punctuation, diacritics, then spacing; Crafting Type's three-day workshop runs anatomy and sketching, then lowercase and punctuation, then "Invisible Type Forms" (spacing), then capitals, numbers, accents and families with a group critique; Type@Cooper and KABK put broad-nib pen and brush work before any digitising and run 9-to-9 days of "drawing, lots and lots of drawing" ([Yale: Designing Type](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300249927/designing-type/); [Crafting Type schedule](http://craftingtype.com/schedule/); [Typographica: Cooper and KABK](https://typographica.org/on-typography/undergrad-to-cooper-to-kabk-one-students-route-to-learning-type-design/); [Type@Cooper Condensed](http://coopertype.org/programs/condensed_program_nyc)). Glyphs' own Getting Started order is drawing good paths → edit view → adding glyphs → glyph names → spacing ([Glyphs Getting Started](https://glyphsapp.com/learn/recommendation:get-started)).

The synthesis usable as workbook gates is: brief (serif or sans, text or display, what is distinctive) → sketch or pen study → control set n o (+ H O), drawn large and spaced together → n-family, o-family, then distinctive letters a e g s to make a starter word → remaining lowercase, caps, figures, punctuation, diacritics → spacing strings and paragraph proofs → kerning → OpenType, hinting, export → family and interpolation, with a printed or rendered proof at each gate. Evidence on how beginners learn to see is thin: one MA thesis (Gosnell 2015) frames the pedagogy as teaching students "to learn to see beyond type's basic formal characteristics," and practitioner accounts credit pen work, repeated drawing of control letters, side-by-side proofs, TypeCooker parameter recipes and public crit; **no controlled study of comparison-based learning in type design was found**, so Typewright's overlay-and-paired-proof learning loop rests on practice, not measurement ([Texas State: Gosnell thesis](https://digital.library.txst.edu/items/91fafb23-20ed-4905-8b42-ca1382edf00c)).

## Automation covers tables and metadata, not taste

The honest coverage statement Typewright should show its users is that fontbakery/Fontspector's googlefonts profile verifies table validity, name/metadata/licence strings, vertical-metrics arithmetic, glyph coverage against the GF glyphsets, shaping regressions for supported languages via shaperglot, and six unit-level outline heuristics, and that "most of the checks are for OpenType binary files, and project metadata files," while Google says type-design standards go "out of the scope" of automation and are reviewed by eye ([fontbakery README](https://github.com/fonttools/fontbakery/blob/main/README.md); [GF Guide: QA tools](https://googlefonts.github.io/gf-guide/qa.html)). ## Conclusion

The research reframes "font quality" for Typewright as three layered contracts rather than one score. The lowest layer, format legality, GF packaging rules, anchor and feature conventions, fontbakery's six outline thresholds, is fully specifiable with numbers and named check IDs and should be enforced silently and deterministically; nearly every first-submission failure on Google Fonts is in this layer and is preventable at export time. The middle layer, extrema, overshoot, node economy, oblique and bold construction, spacing classes, accent placement, has stable practitioner consensus but numbers that are starting points (Glyphs' one-third and 1.5× ratios, Microsoft's 5–10 % gap, FontLab's 6–20 units, the 6–12° oblique range), so the app should surface them as defaults with the source named and let users override. The top layer, texture, rhythm, stroke coherence, script-appropriate proportion, whether a design is any good, has no automation anywhere in the ecosystem, and the most credible thing Typewright can do is reproduce the practitioner loop (control letters, sandwich strings, printed proofs, interleaved comparison, a focused crit question) and say plainly that a green report from fontbakery means "will not break," not "is good."

Two implications follow for an app whose input is scans and drawings. First, the vectoriser is a type-design problem, not a graphics problem: the biggest quality gain comes from forcing extrema and corners as segment boundaries before least-squares fitting and from preserving overshoot that rasterisation destroys, and the newest research (anchor-field vectorisation with near-zero parameter growth under boundary noise) is worth tracking but unlicensed and untested on glyphs. Second, provenance is a design choice made at export: because no QA tool inspects it, Typewright's UFO/designspace output is the only thing that makes a scanned font a legitimate Google Fonts source, and that means components, anchors, feature code and masters must survive the pipeline intact rather than being flattened into outlines that merely pass the checks.
